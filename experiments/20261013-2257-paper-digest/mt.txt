                                                                                     1. Introduction
                                             Throughout this paper, a finite set S ⊂ R is called a Sidon set if all pairwise sums x + y
                                          with x, y ∈ S and x ≤ y are distinct. For a finite set A ⊂ R, let h(A) denote the maximum
                                          cardinality of a Sidon subset of A.
                                             Sidon sets are among the most intensively studied objects in additive combinatorics, owing
                                          to their deep connections with extremal combinatorics, number theory, and discrete geometry;
                                          see, e.g., [10]. Motivated by problems posed by Erdős and by Sárközy and Sós, we study the
                                          asymptotic density of the largest Sidon subsets contained in sets that are close to being Sidon.
                                          We consider this problem under two related but distinct notions of proximity to the Sidon
                                          property, to be elaborated in the following two subsections.

                                          1.1. A problem of Sárközy and Sós on weak Sidon sets. The first notion of proximity
                                          to being Sidon that we consider arises naturally. A finite set A ⊂ R is called a weak Sidon set

                                          and Sós asked how large a Sidon subset must be contained in a weak Sidon set. To state this
                                          formally, for each positive integer n, define
                                                           g(n) := min{ h(A) : A ⊂ R, |A| = n, A is a weak Sidon set }.

1.3. Proof strategy. We outline the main ideas behind the proofs of our results in this
subsection. The proofs of the existence statement in Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.5 are both
based on a subadditivity argument: we show that g(n) and f (n) are subadditive, and the
existence of the limits then follows from Fekete’s lemma.
   We first sketch the proof strategy for Theorem 1.6. For the upper bound c∗ ≤ 4/7, we
construct an explicit 14-point (4, 5)-set whose largest Sidon subset has size 8, verified by
exhaustive enumeration. Combined with the identity c∗ = inf n≥1 f (n)/n from Theorem 1.5,
this already yields c∗ ≤ 4/7. For the lower bound c∗ ≥ 9/17, we build on the A.P.-hypergraph
framework of Gyárfás and Lehel [5], which we now define below.
Definition 1.7 (A.P.-hypergraph). Let A = {a1 , . . . , an } ⊂ R be a finite set, indexed so that
a1 < · · · < an . The A.P.-hypergraph of A is the 3-uniform hypergraph H(A) = (V, E) on
vertex set A whose edges are precisely the triples {ai , aj , ak } for which ai , aj , ak form a 3-term
arithmetic progression, where 1 ≤ i < j < k ≤ n.
   Let α(H) and τ (H) denote the independence number and the transversal number of a
hypergraph H, respectively. When A is a (4, 5)-set, Sidon subsets of A correspond exactly to
independent sets of H(A); in particular, h(A) = α(H(A)) (see Lemma 2.3). Since α(H(A)) +
τ (H(A)) = |V (H(A))| = n, it is equivalent to working with the transversal number τ (H(A)).

2.1. Relations between Sidon sets, (4, 5)-sets, and weak Sidon sets. We first establish
a general relation between Sidon sets, (4, 5)-sets, and weak Sidon sets, as illustrated in Figure 2.


        Sidon sets            ⊊              (4, 5)-sets          ⊊            weak Sidon sets



           Figure 2. Inclusions among Sidon sets, (4, 5)-sets, and weak Sidon sets.

  We prove these implications in the following two propositions and give examples showing
that the inclusions are strict.
Proposition 2.1. Every Sidon set is a (4, 5)-set.
Proof. Let S ⊂ R be a Sidon set, and let x1 , x2 , x3 , x4 ∈ S be four distinct elements. We claim
that the six absolute differences |xi − xj | (1 ≤ i < j ≤ 4) are all distinct.
   Suppose for contradiction that |xi − xj | = |xk − xℓ | for two distinct unordered pairs {i, j} ̸=
{k, ℓ}. Swapping indices within each pair if necessary, we may assume xi > xj and xk > xℓ ,
so that xi − xj = xk − xℓ . Rearranging gives
                                         xi + xℓ = xk + xj .
The unordered pairs {xi , xℓ } and {xk , xj } are distinct (otherwise one would have {xi , xj } =
{xk , xℓ }, contradicting our choice of pairs), so this equality gives two different representations
of the same sum using elements of S, contradicting that S is Sidon. This shows that all six
absolute differences of any four distinct elements in S are distinct. So S is a (4, 5)-set.       □
Proposition 2.2. Every (4, 5)-set is a weak Sidon set.
Proof. Let A ⊂ R be a (4, 5)-set. Suppose, for a contradiction, that A is not weak Sidon. Then
there exist four distinct elements a1 , a2 , a3 , a4 ∈ A such that
                                         a1 + a4 = a2 + a3 .
By rearranging if necessary, we may assume a1 < a2 < a3 < a4 . Then the above equality
implies a4 − a3 = a2 − a1 , so among the six pairwise absolute differences of {a1 , a2 , a3 , a4 }, we
have
                    |a4 − a3 | = |a2 − a1 |   and     |a4 − a2 | = |a3 − a1 |.
Thus at least two distinct values among the six differences are repeated, so there are at most
four distinct absolute differences. This contradicts the (4, 5)-property.                   □
                         LARGEST SIDON SUBSETS IN WEAK SIDON SETS                                  5

   We remark that these two inclusions are strict. For example, the set {1, 2, 3, 5} is weak
Sidon (its six sums x + y with x < y are 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8), but it is not a (4, 5)-set since its six
pairwise absolute differences take only four distinct values. On the other hand, {1, 2, 3, 6} is a
(4, 5)-set, but it is not Sidon since 1 + 3 = 2 + 2.
2.2. A.P.-hypergraphs. We use the A.P.-hypergraph framework of Gyárfás and Lehel [5].
For a finite set A ⊂ R, let H(A) be its A.P.-hypergraph (Definition 1.7). Throughout this
paper, for a hypergraph H we write nH := |V (H)| and mH := |E(H)|, and let α(H) and τ (H)
denote the independence number and transversal number of H, respectively.
   The next three lemmas are stated in [5, p. 109] without proof. For completeness, we include
full proofs. Moreover, we weaken the hypotheses in two of them: in fact, Lemmas 2.3 and 2.4
hold for all weak Sidon sets, not only for (4, 5)-sets.
   For the first lemma, the key observation is that, under the weak Sidon condition, the Sidon
subsets are exactly those subsets that contain no 3-term arithmetic progression.
Lemma 2.3. Let A be a weak Sidon set, and let H = H(A) be its A.P.-hypergraph. For a
subset S ⊆ A, the following are equivalent:
    (1) S is Sidon;
    (2) S contains no 3-term arithmetic progression.
In particular,
                                        h(A) = α(H).
Proof. If S contains a 3-term arithmetic progression {x − d, x, x + d} with d > 0, then
                                    (x − d) + (x + d) = x + x,
so S is not Sidon. Conversely, suppose that S is not Sidon. Then there exist u, v, x, y ∈ S such
that u + v = x + y, but the two representations are different. Reordering within each pair if
necessary, we may assume u ≤ v, x ≤ y with (u, v) ̸= (x, y).
   We first claim that {u, v, x, y} cannot consist of four distinct elements. Indeed, if it did,
write them as a < b < c < d. Since a is the smallest and d is the largest, the only way to split
these four distinct numbers into two pairs with equal sum is a + d = b + c, which contradicts
the fact that A is weak Sidon.
   Hence {u, v, x, y} has at most three distinct elements. It cannot have only two distinct ele-
ments either. Indeed, if the two values are a < b, then the only possibilities with nondecreasing
order are (a, a), (a, b) and (b, b), whose sums are 2a, a + b, and 2b, respectively, and these are
pairwise distinct. Thus u + v = x + y would force (u, v) = (x, y), contrary to our choice of the
two representations. Therefore {u, v, x, y} has exactly three distinct elements, say a < b < c.
It follows that the two representations must be
                                          a + c = b + b,
so a, b, c form a 3-term arithmetic progression. We have proved that S is Sidon if and only if
S contains no 3-term arithmetic progression.
   Finally, a subset of V (H) is independent in H if and only if it contains no edge of H, i.e.,
no 3-term arithmetic progression from A. Therefore h(A) = α(H).                              □

                      3. Existence of the asymptotic constants
   In this section we prove the existence of the limits defining γ∗ and c∗ by establishing the
first assertion of Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.5. We will use the standard Fekete’s lemma for
subadditive sequences.
Lemma 3.1 (Fekete’s lemma). Let (ak )k≥1 be a sequence of real numbers such that am+n ≤
am + an for all m, n ≥ 1. Then the limit limk→∞ ak /k exists and equals inf k≥1 ak /k.
3.1. Weak Sidon sets: existence of the limit. We first treat the weak Sidon set problem
and prove the first assertion of Theorem 1.2. The following lemma is straightforward.
Lemma 3.2. If A ⊂ R is a weak Sidon set, then qA + t := {qa + t : a ∈ A} is a weak Sidon
set for every q > 0 and t ∈ R. Moreover, h(qA + t) = h(A).
Proof. The map x 7→ qx + t is a bijection on R that preserves all additive relations of the
form x + y = u + v (up to scaling and translation). In particular, the sums of distinct pairs in
A are distinct if and only if the corresponding sums in qA + t are distinct. Hence, the weak
Sidon property is preserved. The statement h(qA + t) = h(A) follows similarly, since the Sidon
property is also invariant under affine transformations.                                      □
  Now we establish the key technical lemma needed in the proof of Theorem 1.2.

3.2. (4, 5)-sets: existence of the limit. We now treat the (4, 5)-set setting using an approach
analogous to that of the previous subsection.
Lemma 3.5. Let A ⊂ R be finite, t ∈ R, and q > 0. Set qA + t := {qa + t : a ∈ A}. Then A
is a (4, 5)-set if and only if qA + t is a (4, 5)-set, and h(qA + t) = h(A).

the six pairwise absolute differences are all rescaled by the same factor q. In particular, the

                             4. Sidon subsets in weak Sidon sets
   In Section 3 we established the first assertion of Theorem 1.2, so the constant γ∗ is well
defined. In this section we prove Theorem 1.3 by establishing matching upper and lower
bounds. In particular, this will imply the precise value of γ∗ and thus prove the second
assertion of Theorem 1.2.

4.1. Upper bound on g(n). We begin with the explicit definition of the weak Sidon sets
required for our construction. For an integer n ≥ 2, define
           Xi := 2 n−i 3 i   for 0 ≤ i ≤ n − 1,           Yi := 2 n−i+1 3 i        for 0 ≤ i ≤ n,
and set
                             A2n+1 := {X0 , . . . , Xn−1 } ∪ {Y0 , . . . , Yn }.
Clearly, we have |A2n+1 | = 2n + 1.
   First, we need to show that the sets A2n+1 are indeed weak Sidon. To proceed, we establish
the following two lemmas, which say that sums in A2n+1 are essentially unique. For m ∈ Z>0 ,
let v3 (m) denote the largest integer k ≥ 0 such that 3k | m.
Lemma 4.1. Let a, b ∈ A2n+1 be distinct, and write S = a + b. Assume that v3 (a) ̸= v3 (b).
Then the unordered pair {a, b} is uniquely determined by S.
Proof. Let i := min{v3 (a), v3 (b)}. Since v3 (a) ̸= v3 (b), we have
                             v3 (S) = v3 (a + b) = min{v3 (a), v3 (b)} = i.
Thus i is uniquely determined by S.
   Assume without loss of generality that v3 (a) = i < v3 (b). Then b ≡ 0 (mod 3i+1 ), whereas
a ̸≡ 0 (mod 3i+1 ). Hence
                                S = a + b ≡ a (mod 3i+1 ).
In A2n+1 there are exactly two elements of 3-adic valuation i, namely Xi and Yi , and both are
nonzero modulo 3i+1 . Moreover,
                Xi ≡ 2 n−i · 3i    (mod 3i+1 ),         Yi ≡ 2 n−i+1 · 3i     (mod 3i+1 ),
so Xi ̸≡ Yi (mod 3i+1 ). Since a ∈ {Xi , Yi } and Xi ̸≡ Yi (mod 3i+1 ), the congruence S ≡ a
(mod 3i+1 ) determines a uniquely. Consequently b = S − a is uniquely determined, and hence
the unordered pair {a, b} is uniquely determined by S.                                    □
Lemma 4.2. Let a, b ∈ A2n+1 be distinct, and write S = a + b. Assume that v3 (a) = v3 (b).
Then the unordered pair {a, b} is uniquely determined by S; moreover, there do not exist distinct
c, d ∈ A2n+1 with v3 (c) ̸= v3 (d) such that S = c + d.
Proof. Let i := v3 (a) = v3 (b). Since A2n+1 contains exactly two elements of 3-adic valuation i
for 0 ≤ i ≤ n − 1, namely Xi and Yi , while the valuation n occurs only for Yn , the assumption
a ̸= b forces i ≤ n − 1 and {a, b} = {Xi , Yi }. Hence
                     S = a + b = Xi + Yi = 2 n−i 3i + 2 n−i+1 3i = 2 n−i 3i+1 .
In particular, v3 (S) = i + 1, so i is uniquely determined by S as i = v3 (S) − 1. Thus any
representation of S as a sum of two distinct elements of A2n+1 with equal 3-adic valuation
must be the pair {Xi , Yi }. This proves the first assertion of this lemma.
   It remains to rule out a representation S = c+d with c, d ∈ A2n+1 distinct and v3 (c) ̸= v3 (d).
In that case one has
                                v3 (S) = min{v3 (c), v3 (d)} = i + 1,

so the summand of smaller 3-adic valuation has valuation i + 1. If i = n − 1, then this forces
that summand to be Yn = S, so the other summand equals 0, contradicting 0 ∈       / A2n+1 . Hence
we may assume i ≤ n − 2, so the smaller-valuation summand is either Xi+1 or Yi+1 .
   If it is Yi+1 , then Yi+1 = 2 n−i 3i+1 = S, forcing the other summand to be 0, again contra-
dicting 0 ∈ / A2n+1 . If it is Xi+1 , then Xi+1 = 2 n−i−1 3i+1 = S/2, forcing the other summand
to equal S − Xi+1 = Xi+1 , contradicting distinctness. Hence no such representation exists. □
  Now we can verify that the sets A2n+1 are indeed weak Sidon.
Proposition 4.3. For every n ≥ 2, the set A2n+1 is a weak Sidon set.
Proof. In this proof, we call a pair {a, b} ⊂ A2n+1 type 1 if v3 (a) ̸= v3 (b) and type 2 otherwise.
For a contradiction, let a < b and c < d be two pairs of distinct elements of A2n+1 such that
S := a + b = c + d. By Lemma 4.1 and the first assertion of Lemma 4.2, one of the pairs (say
{a, b}) must be of type 1 and the other pair {c, d} of type 2. However, this contradicts the
second assertion of Lemma 4.2. Hence all sums x + y with x < y in A2n+1 are distinct, i.e.
A2n+1 is weak Sidon.                                                                              □
