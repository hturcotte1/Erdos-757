## Statement

**Conventions** (as in L017). A *3-graph* $K$ has vertex set $V(K)$ and edge set $E(K)$ of 3-subsets; $n(K)$, $m(K)$, $d_K(x)$, $\Delta(K)$ as usual; $\tau(K)$ is the transversal number, a *minimum transversal* is a transversal with $\tau(K)$ vertices, $\mathcal T(K)$ is the set of minimum transversals. $s(K)=3n(K)+2m(K)-11\tau(K)$ is the *slack*; *I11 holds for $K$* iff $s(K)\ge0$; $K$ is *tight* iff $s(K)=0$. For $Z\subseteq V(K)$, $K-Z$ is the induced subgraph on $V(K)\setminus Z$; $\partial_K(Z)$ is the set of edges meeting $Z$; for a set $D$ of edges, $K\setminus D$ deletes the edges of $D$ and keeps all vertices. A set $X$ is *co-transversal in $K$* if $X\subseteq T$ for some $T\in\mathcal T(K)$. Two vertices are *adjacent in $K$* if they lie in a common edge of $K$; a *common neighbour* of $u\ne w$ is a vertex $t\notin\{u,w\}$ with $t$ adjacent to both. $e_K(S)$ is the number of edges of $K$ inside $S$. $C$ is the class of 3-graphs that are (P1) linear, (P2) $F_7$-free ($F_7=\{012,034,056,135,246\}$) and (P3) satisfy $e(S)\le|S|-2$ for every $S$ with $|S|\ge2$. A *minimal counterexample* is an $H\in C$ with $s(H)<0$ such that every member of $C$ with fewer vertices satisfies I11. A set $S$ with $|S|\ge3$ and $e_H(S)=|S|-2$ is *saturated* (for $H\in C$ this is the largest value (P3) allows; 'saturated' as in 'midpoint-saturated', $m=n-2$, of START_HERE; not to be confused with *tight*, which refers to the slack).

**Gadgets.** For a 3-graph $K$ and pairwise disjoint 2-subsets $Q_1,\dots,Q_k$ of $V(K)$, $K+w(Q_1,\dots,Q_k)$ is the 3-graph obtained by adding a new vertex $w$ and the $k$ edges $\{w\}\cup Q_j$. A set $T$ *meets* $Q_1,\dots,Q_k$ if $T\cap Q_j\ne\emptyset$ for all $j$.

**Folds.** Let $K$ be a linear 3-graph and $x$ a vertex with $d_K(x)=2$, lying in the edges $\{x,u,u'\}$ and $\{x,w,w'\}$ (the five vertices $x,u,u',w,w'$ are distinct by linearity). For $y\in\{u,u'\}$ and $z\in\{w,w'\}$ not adjacent in $K$, the *fold* $K^\circ=K^\circ_{x;y,z}$ is obtained from $K-x$ by identifying $y$ and $z$ into one new vertex $\zeta$; $\varphi:V(K)\setminus\{x\}\to V(K^\circ)$ denotes the identification map ($\varphi(y)=\varphi(z)=\zeta$, identity elsewhere), and $E(K^\circ)=\{\varphi(g):g\in E(K-x)\}$. The fold is *blocked in $C$* if $K^\circ\notin C$; there are four folds at $x$ (one for each non-adjacent pair $(y,z)$; adjacent pairs give no fold and are also called blocked).

**Conjecture C1** (experiments/20261027-0300-transversal-richness, README "Conjectures"). Let $F\in C$ be connected with $s(F)\le1$, such that I11 holds for every member of $C$ with at most $n_F:=n(F)$ vertices. Let $P_1=\{a,b\}$, $P_2=\{c,d\}$, $P_3=\{e,f\}$ be pairwise disjoint 2-subsets of $V(F)$ with $H:=F+v(P_1,P_2,P_3)\in C$. Then some minimum transversal of $F$ meets $P_1,P_2,P_3$. We call this *the setting of C1* (it implies $s(F)\in\{0,1\}$).

**Theorem 1 (slack floor at the first open size).** Let $N\ge0$ and suppose every member of $C$ with at most $N$ vertices satisfies I11. Let $K\in C$ with $n(K)=N+1$ and $s(K)<0$. Then $K$ is a minimal counterexample; for every vertex $x$ of $K$, $x$ is co-transversal in $K$ and $s(K-x)=s(K)+8-2d_K(x)\ge0$; $\Delta(K)=3$; and $s(K)\in\{-1,-2\}$. In particular every $K\in C$ with $N+1$ vertices has $s(K)\ge-2$, and $s(K)\ge0$ if $\Delta(K)\ge4$ or $\Delta(K)\le2$.

**Theorem 2 (richness).** Suppose every member of $C$ with at most $N$ vertices satisfies I11. Let $K\in C$, $k\in\{1,2,3\}$, and let $Q_1,\dots,Q_k$ be pairwise disjoint 2-subsets of $V(K)$ with $K^+:=K+w(Q_1,\dots,Q_k)\in C$. Each of the following conditions implies that some minimum transversal of $K$ meets $Q_1,\dots,Q_k$:
 (i) $n(K)\le N-1$ and $s(K)\le7-2k$ (that is $s(K)\le5,3,1$ for $k=1,2,3$);
 (ii) $n(K)=N$ and $s(K)\le5-2k$ (that is $s(K)\le3,1$ for $k=1,2$; vacuous for $k=3$);
 (iii) $n(K)=N$, $s(K)\le7-2k$, and $\Delta(K^+)\ge4$ (for instance some vertex of $Q_1\cup\dots\cup Q_k$ has degree 3 in $K$, or $\Delta(K)\ge4$).
 (iv) (edge deletion) If $n(K)\le N$ then $\tau(K\setminus D)\le\tau(K)-\lceil(2|D|-s(K))/11\rceil$ for every set $D$ of edges; if $n(K)=N+1$ then $\tau(K\setminus D)\le\tau(K)-\lceil(2|D|-s(K)-2)/11\rceil$. In particular every edge $g$ of $K$ is critical ($\tau(K\setminus g)=\tau(K)-1$) if $n(K)\le N$ and $s(K)\le1$, or if $n(K)=N+1$ and $s(K)<0$.
Consequences for a minimal counterexample $H$ with $n$ vertices (apply with $N=n-1$): for every vertex $y$ with $d_H(y)=3$, $H-y$ is pair-rich (two disjoint pairs $Q_1,Q_2$ with $(H-y)+w(Q_1,Q_2)\in C$ are met by some minimum transversal of $H-y$) and triple-rich for triples with a degree-4 witness ((iii) with $K=H-y$, $s(K)=s(H)+2\le1$); for every vertex $y$ with $d_H(y)=2$, $H-y$ is pair-rich for pairs with a degree-4 witness ($s(H-y)=s(H)+4\le3$); every member of $C$ with at most $n-2$ vertices and slack $\le1$ (resp. $\le3$) is triple-rich (resp. pair-rich); and every edge of $H$ is critical (for single edges this is the conclusion of L017 Theorem 1(g), obtained here without its extra assumption that $m(H)$ is least; for larger $D$ the bound of (iv) is weaker by $2/11$ than the one there).

**Theorem 3 (pair-pair coverage in the setting of C1).** In the setting of C1, for any two of the three pairs, say $P_i,P_j$, some minimum transversal of $F$ meets $P_i$ and $P_j$. (No assumption on the third pair beyond $H\in C$ is needed: it suffices that $F+v(P_i,P_j)\in C$.)

**Theorem 4 (what C1 is).** In the setting of C1:
 (a) The following are equivalent: (1) C1 fails for $F,P_1,P_2,P_3$; (2) $\tau(H)=\tau(F)+1$; (3) $v$ is co-transversal in $H$; (4) $s(H)<0$; (5) $s(H)=s(F)-2$. (Otherwise $\tau(H)=\tau(F)$ and $s(H)=s(F)+9$.)
 (b) If C1 fails, then $H$ is a minimal counterexample with $n(H)=n_F+1$ and $s(H)=s(F)-2\in\{-2,-1\}$, $v$ is a vertex of degree 3 of $H$ and $H-v=F$ is connected: this is case (a) of L017 Theorem 4. Conversely, for every minimal counterexample $H$ and every vertex $v$ of degree 3 with $H-v$ connected, $F:=H-v$ with the three co-vertex pairs of $v$ is in the setting of C1 and C1 fails for it. So C1 holds for all admissible data if and only if no minimal counterexample has a vertex $v$ of degree 3 with $H-v$ connected.
 (c) C1 holds (for $F$ and the three pairs) whenever $n_F\le26$; whenever $\Delta(F)\ge4$; and whenever some vertex of $P_1\cup P_2\cup P_3$ has degree $\ge3$ in $F$.
 (d) If C1 fails, then for each $\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}$ the family $\mathcal T_{ij}$ of minimum transversals of $F$ meeting $P_i$ and $P_j$ is nonempty (Theorem 3) and every $T\in\mathcal T_{ij}$ is disjoint from $P_k$; in particular the three families are pairwise disjoint.

**Theorem 5 (folding lemma).** Let $K$ be a linear 3-graph, $x$ a vertex of degree 2 with edges $\{x,u,u'\}$, $\{x,w,w'\}$, and $y\in\{u,u'\}$, $z\in\{w,w'\}$ non-adjacent in $K$; let $K^\circ=K^\circ_{x;y,z}$.
 (a) $K^\circ$ is a 3-graph with $n(K^\circ)=n(K)-2$ and $m(K^\circ)=m(K)-2$ ($\varphi$ is injective on $E(K-x)$ and no image edge is degenerate), and $\Delta(K^\circ)\le\max(\Delta(K),d_K(y)+d_K(z)-2)$.
 (b) $\tau(K)\le\tau(K^\circ)+1$.
 (c) If $x$ is co-transversal in $K$, then $\tau(K^\circ)=\tau(K)-1$ and $s(K^\circ)=s(K)+1$.
 (d) Suppose $K\in C$. Then: $K^\circ$ is linear iff $y$ and $z$ have no common neighbour in $K-x$; every copy of $F_7$ in $K^\circ$ contains $\zeta$; and $K^\circ$ satisfies (P3) iff there is no saturated set $S\subseteq V(K)\setminus\{x\}$ of $K$ with $y,z\in S$; such a set has $|S|\ge6$.
 (e) Consequently, if $H$ is a minimal counterexample with $s(H)=-2$, every fold of $H$ is blocked in $C$: for every vertex $x$ of degree 2 and every non-adjacent pair $(y,z)$ as above, $y$ and $z$ have a common neighbour in $H-x$, or $H^\circ$ contains an $F_7$ through $\zeta$, or $H$ has a saturated set $S\not\ni x$ containing $y$ and $z$. If $s(H)=-1$, every fold of $H$ that lies in $C$ is a tight member of $C$ with $n(H)-2$ vertices.

**Theorem 6 (C1 for tight $F$: reduction to blocked folds).** Assume the setting of C1 with $s(F)=0$ and that C1 fails; so $H=F+v(P_1,P_2,P_3)$ is a minimal counterexample with $s(H)=-2$ (Theorem 4). Then:
 (a) every vertex of $H$ has degree 2 or 3, and $H$ has exactly $3(n(H)-m(H))\ge6$ vertices of degree 2;
 (b) for every vertex $x$ of degree 2 with edges $\{x,u,u'\}$, $\{x,w,w'\}$, any $y\in\{u,u'\}$ and $z\in\{w,w'\}$ with $d_H(y)=d_H(z)=3$ are adjacent in $H-x$;
 (c) every fold of $H$ is blocked in $C$ (Theorem 5(e)); in particular the four folds at each of the $\ge6$ vertices of degree 2 are blocked;
 (d) let $x\in P_i$ be a co-vertex of $v$ with $d_H(x)=2$, $\{x,p,q\}$ its edge in $F$ and $\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}$. Then every $t\in\{p,q\}$ with $t\notin P_j\cup P_k$ has $d_H(t)=2$; and, writing $g_t$ for the second edge of such a $t$, the fold $H^\circ_{x;v,t}$ is blocked in $C$ if and only if $g_t$ meets $P_j\cup P_k$, or $H^\circ_{x;v,t}$ contains an $F_7$ through $\zeta$, or $H$ has a saturated set $U$ with $v\in U$, $x,t\notin U$ and $g_t\setminus\{t\}\subseteq U$.
Hence: **C1 holds for every tight $F$ for which $H=F+v(P_1,P_2,P_3)$ has a fold that lies in $C$**, and, more generally, for every tight $F$ for which $H$ violates one of (a), (b), (d).

**Lemma 7 (swap lemma).** In the setting of C1, suppose C1 fails, let $\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}$, $T\in\mathcal T_{ij}$, $t\in P_k$, and let $g$ be an edge of $F$ containing $t$ with $T\cap g=\{y\}$. If $g$ is the only edge $h$ of $F$ with $h\cap T=\{y\}$, then $T\cap P_i=\{y\}$ or $T\cap P_j=\{y\}$.

**Proposition 8 (no single exact step decides C1 when $s(F)=1$).** Let $H$ be a minimal counterexample, $s=s(H)$. For each operation below, the resulting 3-graph $H'$ (if it lies in $C$) has the stated number of vertices and slack:

| operation | $n(H')$ | $s(H')$ |
|---|---|---|
| delete a vertex of degree $d$ | $n-1$ | $s+8-2d$ (exact; $\ge s+2$) |
| delete a co-transversal set $X$ | $n-\lvert X\rvert$ | $s+8\lvert X\rvert-2\lvert\partial_H(X)\rvert\ \ge s+2\lvert X\rvert$ (exact) |
| delete a set $D\ne\emptyset$ of edges | $n$ | $s-2\lvert D\rvert+11(\tau(H)-\tau(H\setminus D))\ \ge-2$ |
| fold at a vertex of degree 2 | $n-2$ | $s+1$ (exact) |
| replace a degree-3 vertex $y$ by a new vertex with $k\le3$ edges | $n$ | $s+2k-6$ or $s+2k+5$ |

For $s=-1$ every entry with fewer than $n$ vertices has slack $\ge0$ and every entry with $n$ vertices has slack $\ge-2$; so none of these operations, applied once, produces a contradiction with the minimality of $H$. For $s=-2$ the fold (slack $-1$, $n-2$ vertices) is the only entry that does (Theorem 5(e)).

**Proposition 9 (folds when $s=-1$, conditional on Conjecture T3).** Assume Conjecture T3 (L017 13.1: every connected tight member of $C$ with maximum degree $\le3$ has at most 24 vertices). Let $H$ be a minimal counterexample with $s(H)=-1$ and $x$ a vertex of degree 2 such that $H-x$ is connected. Then every fold $H^\circ_{x;y,z}$ with $d_H(y)+d_H(z)\le5$ is blocked in $C$. (By L017 Theorem 5(A)(iv), $H-x$ is connected unless a co-vertex of $x$ has degree 1; under T3 there is no vertex of degree 1 at all, by L017 Corollary 13.5(i), and every minimal counterexample has $s=-1$, by L017 Corollary 13.3(iii).)

**Proposition 10 (the closed neighbourhood of $v$ is expensive to cover).** Let $H$ be a minimal counterexample, $v$ a vertex of degree 3 with co-vertex pairs $P_1,P_2,P_3$, $W=P_1\cup P_2\cup P_3$, $Z=\{v\}\cup W$, and $k$ the number of edges of $H-v$ meeting $W$. Then $k\ge3$, and every set $T$ meeting all edges of $H$ that meet $Z$ satisfies $11|T|\ge27+2k-s(H)$; in particular $|T|\ge4$, and $|T|\ge5$ if $k\ge9$ (or $k\ge8$ when $s(H)=-2$). In words: no three vertices meet all edges at $v$ and all edges at its six co-vertices. (This holds for every degree-3 vertex, in particular in case (a); it is an instance of the reduction (R) of L017 together with $F_7$-freeness.)

**Lemma 11 (saturated sets when $\Delta\le3$: six ports).** Let $K\in C$ with $\Delta(K)\le3$ and let $S$ be a saturated set of $K$. Then (a) $\sum_{y\in S}(3-d_{K[S]}(y))=6$; (b) every $y\in S$ has $d_{K[S]}(y)\ge1$, and if $d_{K[S]}(y)=1$ and $|S|\ge4$ then $S\setminus\{y\}$ is saturated; (c) if every vertex of $S$ has $K[S]$-degree $\ge2$ (we call $S$ *2-core*), then exactly six vertices of $S$ (its *ports*) have $K[S]$-degree 2 and every other vertex of $S$ has all its edges inside $S$; so at most six edges of $K$ meet both $S$ and $V(K)\setminus S$, each through a port. Repeatedly deleting vertices of $K[S]$-degree 1 turns every saturated set into a single edge or a 2-core saturated set.

## Proof

### Step 0. Facts used
0.1 (Additivity; L017 0.1.) $n,m,\tau,s$ are additive over disjoint unions; an isolated vertex has $s=3$, a single edge has $s=0$, the empty 3-graph has $s=0$.

0.2 (Heredity; L017 0.2.) Deleting vertices or edges preserves (P1), (P2), (P3), hence membership in $C$.

0.3 For $Z\subseteq V(K)$: $\tau(K)-|Z|\le\tau(K-Z)\le\tau(K)$ (if $T$ is a transversal of $K$, $T\setminus Z$ is one of $K-Z$; if $T'$ is one of $K-Z$, $T'\cup Z$ is one of $K$).

0.4 $X$ is co-transversal in $K$ iff $\tau(K-X)=\tau(K)-|X|$ (a minimum transversal $T\supseteq X$ gives the transversal $T\setminus X$ of $K-X$; conversely a minimum transversal $T'$ of $K-X$ with $\tau(K)-|X|$ elements is disjoint from $X$ and $T'\cup X$ is a minimum transversal of $K$). For a single vertex: $x$ co-transversal iff $\tau(K-x)=\tau(K)-1$.

0.5 (Slack identity.) $s(K-Z)=s(K)-3|Z|-2|\partial_K(Z)|+11(\tau(K)-\tau(K-Z))$ and $s(K\setminus D)=s(K)-2|D|+11(\tau(K)-\tau(K\setminus D))$.

0.6 (Linearity.) Two distinct vertices of a linear 3-graph lie in at most one common edge; the co-vertices of a vertex in its different edges are distinct; $|\partial_K(\{x,y\})|=d_K(x)+d_K(y)-\sigma$, $\sigma\in\{0,1\}$ the number of edges containing both.

0.7 (Gadget transversals.) Let $K^+=K+w(Q_1,\dots,Q_k)$. Then $\tau(K)\le\tau(K^+)\le\tau(K)+1$, and $\tau(K^+)=\tau(K)$ iff some minimum transversal of $K$ meets $Q_1,\dots,Q_k$. *Proof.* $K^+-w=K$ gives the first two inequalities (0.3). If $T\in\mathcal T(K)$ meets all $Q_j$, $T$ is a transversal of $K^+$. Conversely let $T^+$ be a transversal of $K^+$ with $\tau(K)$ elements. If $w\in T^+$, then $T^+\setminus\{w\}$ is a transversal of $K$ with $\tau(K)-1$ elements, impossible; so $w\notin T^+$, $T^+\subseteq V(K)$ is a minimum transversal of $K$, and it meets every $Q_j$ (it meets the edges $\{w\}\cup Q_j$). $\square$ Also $n(K^+)=n(K)+1$, $m(K^+)=m(K)+k$, so $s(K^+)=s(K)+3+2k-11(\tau(K^+)-\tau(K))$.

0.8 (L017 Theorem 7; I11 for maximum degree $\le2$.) If $K$ is a linear 3-graph with $\Delta(K)\le2$, then $s(K)\ge0$. *Proof.* Let $n_i$ be the number of vertices of degree $i$. Let $G$ be the graph with vertex set $E(K)$ and one edge $gh$ for every vertex of degree 2 of $K$ lying in $g$ and $h$; $G$ is simple (two vertices of degree 2 in the same two edges $g\ne h$ would give $|g\cap h|\ge2$) with $\Delta(G)\le3$ and $|E(G)|=n_2$. For a maximum matching $M$ of $G$, the $|M|$ vertices of $K$ giving the edges of $M$ meet $2|M|$ edges of $K$; adding one vertex from each of the other $m-2|M|$ edges gives a transversal, so $\tau(K)\le m-\nu(G)$. Hence, using $3m=n_1+2n_2$ and $n=n_0+n_1+n_2$, $s(K)\ge3n+2m-11m+11\nu(G)=3n_0-3n_2+11\nu(G)=3n_0+11\nu(G)-3|E(G)|$. It remains to show $11\nu(G)\ge3|E(G)|$. By the Tutte–Berge formula there is $U\subseteq V(G)$ with $2\nu(G)=|V(G)|+|U|-o(G-U)$, $o$ the number of odd components of $G-U$. Let $C_1,\dots,C_q$ be the components of $G-U$ with $c_i$ vertices and $e_i$ edges; at most $3|U|$ edges meet $U$, so $|E(G)|\le3|U|+\sum e_i$, and $|V(G)|=|U|+\sum c_i$. Then $2\nu(G)-\frac6{11}|E(G)|\ge\frac4{11}|U|+\sum_i t_i$ with $t_i=c_i-[c_i\text{ odd}]-\frac6{11}e_i$. As $C_i$ is simple with maximum degree $\le3$, $e_i\le\min(\binom{c_i}2,\lfloor3c_i/2\rfloor)$, which gives $t_i=0$ for $c_i=1$, $t_i\ge2-\frac6{11}$ for $c_i=2$, $\ge2-\frac{18}{11}$ for $c_i=3$, $\ge4-\frac{36}{11}$ for $c_i=4$, $\ge4-\frac{42}{11}$ for $c_i=5$, $\ge c_i-\frac{9c_i}{11}$ for even $c_i\ge6$, and $\ge c_i-1-\frac{9c_i-3}{11}=\frac{2c_i-8}{11}$ for odd $c_i\ge7$; all are $\ge0$. So $11\nu(G)\ge3|E(G)|$ and $s(K)\ge3n_0\ge0$. $\square$ (L017 Step 4 also proves $\tau(K)=m-\nu(G)$ and the equality cases; they are not needed here.)

### Step 1. Proof of Theorem 1
1.1 $K\in C$, $s(K)<0$, and every member of $C$ with fewer than $N+1$ vertices satisfies I11; so $K$ is a minimal counterexample by definition. (If $N=0$, $K$ is a single vertex with $s=3$; so $N\ge1$ here.)

1.2 Let $x\in V(K)$. $K-x\in C$ (0.2) has $N$ vertices, so $s(K-x)\ge0$. By 0.3, $\tau(K-x)\in\{\tau(K)-1,\tau(K)\}$. If $\tau(K-x)=\tau(K)$, then 0.5 gives $s(K-x)=s(K)-3-2d_K(x)<0$, a contradiction. So $\tau(K-x)=\tau(K)-1$, $x$ is co-transversal (0.4), and $s(K-x)=s(K)-3-2d_K(x)+11=s(K)+8-2d_K(x)\ge0$.

1.3 From 1.2, $2d_K(x)\le s(K)+8\le7$, so $\Delta(K)\le3$. If $\Delta(K)\le2$, 0.8 ($K$ is linear) gives $s(K)\ge0$, a contradiction; so $\Delta(K)=3$, and 1.2 at a vertex of degree 3 gives $s(K)\ge-2$. So $s(K)\in\{-1,-2\}$. The last sentence of the theorem is the contrapositive. $\square$

### Step 2. Proof of Theorem 2
2.1 Suppose no minimum transversal of $K$ meets $Q_1,\dots,Q_k$. By 0.7, $\tau(K^+)=\tau(K)+1$ and $s(K^+)=s(K)+3+2k-11=s(K)+2k-8$. $K^+\in C$ by hypothesis, $n(K^+)=n(K)+1$.
 (i) $n(K^+)\le N$, so $s(K^+)\ge0$, i.e. $s(K)\ge8-2k$, contradicting $s(K)\le7-2k$.
 (ii) $n(K^+)=N+1$, so Theorem 1 gives $s(K^+)\ge-2$, i.e. $s(K)\ge6-2k$, contradicting $s(K)\le5-2k$.

### Step 5. Proof of Theorem 5 (folding lemma)
5.1 (a) $y\ne z$ and they are not adjacent, so no edge of $K-x$ contains both; hence $\varphi(g)$ has 3 elements for every $g\in E(K-x)$. If $\varphi(g)=\varphi(h)$ for edges $g\ne h$ of $K-x$, then $\zeta\in\varphi(g)$ (otherwise $g=h$), so one of $g,h$ contains $y$ and the other $z$ (if both contained $y$, $g\setminus\{y\}=h\setminus\{y\}$ and $|g\cap h|=3$), say $y\in g$, $z\in h$, and $g\setminus\{y\}=h\setminus\{z\}$ has two elements common to $g$ and $h$, contradicting linearity. So $\varphi$ is injective on $E(K-x)$, $m(K^\circ)=m(K-x)=m(K)-2$ (the edges at $x$ are exactly the two edges at $x$), and $n(K^\circ)=n(K)-2$. $d_{K^\circ}(\zeta)=d_{K-x}(y)+d_{K-x}(z)=d_K(y)+d_K(z)-2$, other degrees are at most those in $K$. $\square$

5.2 (b) Let $T^\circ\in\mathcal T(K^\circ)$. If $\zeta\in T^\circ$, put $T=(T^\circ\setminus\{\zeta\})\cup\{y,z\}$; if $\zeta\notin T^\circ$, put $T=T^\circ\cup\{x\}$. In both cases $|T|=|T^\circ|+1$. $T$ is a transversal of $K$: an edge $g$ of $K-x$ has $\varphi(g)\cap T^\circ\ne\emptyset$; if this intersection contains a vertex $t\ne\zeta$, then $t\in g\cap T$; if it is $\{\zeta\}$, then $\zeta\in T^\circ$, $g$ contains $y$ or $z$, and both lie in $T$. The edges $\{x,u,u'\}\ni y$ and $\{x,w,w'\}\ni z$ are met by $\{y,z\}$ in the first case and by $x$ in the second. So $\tau(K)\le\tau(K^\circ)+1$. $\square$

5.3 (c) Let $T\in\mathcal T(K)$ with $x\in T$. Then $T\setminus\{x\}$ meets every edge of $K-x$, so $\varphi(T\setminus\{x\})$ (which has at most $|T|-1$ elements) meets every edge $\varphi(g)$ of $K^\circ$. Hence $\tau(K^\circ)\le\tau(K)-1$, and with (b) $\tau(K^\circ)=\tau(K)-1$; then $s(K^\circ)=3(n-2)+2(m-2)-11(\tau(K)-1)=s(K)+1$. $\square$

5.4 (d) *Linearity.* Let $g\ne h$ be edges of $K-x$ with $|\varphi(g)\cap\varphi(h)|\ge2$. If $\zeta\notin\varphi(g)\cap\varphi(h)$, the common elements are common to $g$ and $h$: impossible. So $\zeta\in\varphi(g)\cap\varphi(h)$ together with some $t\ne\zeta$; then $t\notin\{y,z\}$ and $t\in g\cap h$. If $g,h$ both contain $y$ (or both $z$), $|g\cap h|\ge2$: impossible. So, say, $y\in g$, $z\in h$ (and $z\notin g$, $y\notin h$, as $y,z$ are not adjacent), and $t\in g\cap h$ is a common neighbour of $y$ and $z$ in $K-x$. Conversely a common neighbour $t$ in edges $g\ni y,t$ and $h\ni z,t$ ($g\ne h$, since $y,z$ are not adjacent) gives $\{\zeta,t\}\subseteq\varphi(g)\cap\varphi(h)$ with $\varphi(g)\ne\varphi(h)$ (5.1). *$F_7$.* A copy of $F_7$ in $K^\circ$ avoiding $\zeta$ consists of edges $\varphi(g)=g$ with $g\subseteq V(K)\setminus\{x,y,z\}$, i.e. it is a copy in $K$: impossible as $K\in C$. *(P3).* Let $S^\circ\subseteq V(K^\circ)$, $|S^\circ|\ge2$. If $\zeta\notin S^\circ$, the edges of $K^\circ$ inside $S^\circ$ are edges of $K$ inside $S^\circ$, at most $|S^\circ|-2$. If $\zeta\in S^\circ$, put $S=(S^\circ\setminus\{\zeta\})\cup\{y,z\}=\varphi^{-1}(S^\circ)$, $|S|=|S^\circ|+1\ge3$, $x\notin S$; since $\varphi(g)\subseteq S^\circ$ iff $g\subseteq S$ (for $g\in E(K-x)$) and $\varphi$ is injective on edges, $e_{K^\circ}(S^\circ)=e_{K-x}(S)=e_K(S)\le|S|-2=|S^\circ|-1$. So (P3) fails for $K^\circ$ iff some such $S$ has $e_K(S)=|S|-2$, i.e. is saturated. Finally, a saturated $S\ni y,z$ has $|S|\ge6$: $|S|=3$ means $S$ is an edge containing $y,z$ (they are not adjacent); $|S|=4$ needs two edges inside a 4-set, which share two vertices; $|S|=5$ needs three edges inside a 5-set, which is impossible for a linear 3-graph (two 3-subsets of a 5-set meet in $\ge1$ point, so in exactly one; with $g_1=\{1,2,3\}$, $g_2=\{1,4,5\}$ a third edge would meet each in exactly one point: if it contains 1 its other two points avoid $\{2,3,4,5\}$, and otherwise it has one point in $\{2,3\}$ and one in $\{4,5\}$ only). $\square$

5.5 (e) Let $H$ be a minimal counterexample. Every vertex is co-transversal (Theorem 1 with $N=n-1$), so by (c) every fold has $s(H^\circ)=s(H)+1$ and $n(H^\circ)=n-2$. If $s(H)=-2$ and $H^\circ\in C$, then $H^\circ$ is a member of $C$ with fewer vertices than $H$ and slack $-1$: impossible. So $H^\circ\notin C$, and by (d) one of the three listed alternatives holds (non-adjacency of $y,z$ is part of the definition of a fold). If $s(H)=-1$, $s(H^\circ)=0$. $\square$

Inside a minimal counterexample such a piece $S\ne V(H)$ has at most six boundary edges, and by the (R+) reduction of L017 no minimum transversal of $H[S]$ meets all of them; the gadget method of Theorem 2 cannot be used to cover them, because attaching a new vertex to the ports violates (P3) on $S\cup\{w\}$ as soon as it has two edges into $S$ ($e(S\cup\{w\})\ge|S|$). This is where (P3) is exactly tight and where a further reduction for GAP 2 would have to act.

## GAP
Conjecture C1 is not proved. By Theorem 4(b), C1 is equivalent to: **no minimal counterexample $H$ has a vertex $v$ of degree 3 with $H-v$ connected**. What is proved for such an $H$ (if it exists): $n(H)\ge28$ (L016), all of L017 Theorems 1–5 (unrefereed); Theorems 1–3 here (slack floor; richness; pair-pair coverage for every two of the three pairs, so only genuine three-pair obstructions remain, Theorem 4(d)); Lemma 7 for every member of $\mathcal T_{12}\cup\mathcal T_{13}\cup\mathcal T_{23}$.

**GAP 1 ($s(F)=1$, i.e. $s(H)=-1$).** Exclude a minimal counterexample $H$ with $s(H)=-1$ and a degree-3 vertex $v$ such that $H-v$ is connected. Obstruction: Proposition 8 — every exact single operation available (vertex and co-transversal-set deletions, edge deletions, folds, one-vertex gadget replacements) yields slack $\ge0$ on fewer vertices or slack $\ge-2$ on $n$ vertices. No local configuration argument of the kind used in L017/L018 or here can close this case without an additional structural input. One such input is Conjecture T3: by Proposition 9 it turns folds into a tool for $s=-1$ as well (folds with degree sum $\le5$ at degree-2 vertices $x$ with $H-x$ connected must be blocked), reducing GAP 1 to the same blocked-fold configurations as GAP 2, plus folds of two degree-3 vertices.

**GAP 2 ($s(F)=0$, i.e. $s(H)=-2$).** By Theorem 6 it suffices to show that a minimal counterexample $H$ with $s(H)=-2$ (degrees 2 and 3 only, at least six vertices of degree 2) has a vertex of degree 2 with a fold in $C$, or to find a further exact reduction for the configurations in which all folds are blocked. This is a statement about all minimal counterexamples with $s=-2$ (it does not use the vertex $v$; if true it would also show that every minimal counterexample has $s=-1$, the conclusion of L017 Corollary 13.3 under Conjecture T3). It cannot be proved by a purely local argument from folds: computation C5/C6 shows that the two classes of cell$(14,12,6)$ with $\Delta=3$ (tight members of $C$, six vertices of degree 2) have all 24 folds blocked, every one of them already by adjacency or a common neighbour (non-linearity), so the configuration "all folds blocked" occurs in $C$ at small size; and C7 shows that even together with Theorem 6(a),(b) at every vertex of degree 2 it occurs (three classes of $(10,8,4)$, slack 2, every fold blocked by adjacency or a common neighbour, i.e. by local reasons, no saturated set needed). A proof of GAP 2 must therefore use $s(H)=-2$ beyond Theorem 6(a),(b),(c), for example through further reductions at these configurations (in the style of the special hypergraphs of Henning–Yeo arXiv:1802.01825 §7). *Conditionally*, GAP 2 is closed by a finiteness statement for tight graphs: if C1 failed for a tight $F$, then $F=H-v$ would be a connected tight member of $C$ with $\Delta(F)\le3$ (Theorem 1) and $n_F\ge27$ vertices (Theorem 4(c)); so C1 holds for every tight $F$ as soon as no connected tight member of $C$ with maximum degree $\le3$ has exactly $n_F$ vertices — in particular for all tight $F$ under Conjecture T3 (this is the argument of L017 Corollary 13.3(iii) specialised to case (a)). Under T3 the essential open case of C1 is therefore GAP 1 (slack-1 $F$), where Proposition 9 is the available tool.

**On the data behind C1.** For $n_F\le26$, C1 holds by L016 (Theorem 4(c)); therefore every uncoverable triple in the stored lists necessarily has $F+v\notin C$ (this much is forced by L016; the particular form of the observed witnesses, an $F_7$ and a set of 8 or 9 vertices with $e(S)=|S|-1$, experiments/20261027-0300-transversal-richness items 4–5, is not), and these witnesses do not by themselves indicate a local mechanism for $n_F\ge27$. In a minimal counterexample the failure of C1 is equivalent to $v$ being co-transversal (Theorem 4(a)(3)), which holds for every vertex of every minimal counterexample (Theorem 1); it is a global property.

## Relation to the strategy proposed for C1
(1) Pair-pair coverage: proved (Theorem 3), not by the slack trick (which only gives the pair lemma $d_x+d_y-\sigma\le3$, no contradiction) but by the gadget $H\setminus(\{v\}\cup P_k)$ and the slack floor of Theorem 1. (2) Exchange structure: Lemma 7 is the exact exchange statement; it does not close the case. (3) "Uncoverable $\Rightarrow$ dense": in a minimal counterexample uncoverability is automatic (Theorem 4(a)(3) and Theorem 1), so density must come from reductions; the fold is the only exact reduction with gain $+1$ (Proposition 8), and its failure in $C$ is equivalent to adjacency, a common neighbour, an $F_7$ through the merged vertex, or a saturated set (Theorem 5(d)) — this is the precise form in which density appears. (4) Extra hypotheses: C1 is proved when $n_F\le26$, $\Delta(F)\ge4$, a co-vertex has $F$-degree $\ge3$ (Theorem 4(c)), or $F$ is tight and $F+v$ has a fold in $C$ or violates Theorem 6(a),(b),(d); the remaining cases are GAP 1 and GAP 2, with the test of GAP 2 being "does every fold of $H$ leave $C$" (computable by `fold_pergraph.py`, together with the Theorem 6(b) test of `blocked_and_6b.py`, on any candidate). Under Conjecture T3 the tight case is closed outright (GAP 2, last paragraph), and C1 reduces to GAP 1.
