## Proof

### Step 0. Conventions and the finite facts
0.1 *(Definitions, kb/00_START_HERE.md §1.)* A finite $A\subset\mathbb R$ is a (4,5)-set if every 4 distinct points of $A$ determine at least 5 distinct distances $|x-y|$; for a 4-set $B$, $B-B=\{0\}\cup\{\pm\delta\}$ over its positive differences $\delta$, so $|B-B|=1+2\cdot\#\{\text{distinct positive differences}\}$, and "$\ge5$ distinct differences" is the same as $|B-B|\ge11$. $S\subseteq A$ is Sidon if the sums $x+y$ ($x\le y$, $x,y\in S$) are pairwise distinct, i.e. if $S$ contains no relation. $h(A)$ is the largest size of a Sidon subset, $f(n)=\min\{h(A):|A|=n,\ A\text{ a (4,5)-set}\}$.

0.2 *(No point on both sides.)* In a relation $(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)$, $p_i\ne p_k$ for $i\in\{1,2\}$, $k\in\{3,4\}$: e.g. $p_1=p_3$ forces $p_2=p_4$ and then $\{p_1,p_2\}=\{p_3,p_4\}$; the other three cases are the same.

0.3 *(Affine invariance.)* For $\lambda\ne0$, $x\mapsto\lambda x+\mu$ maps relations to relations and back and multiplies every distance by $|\lambda|$; so it preserves the (4,5)-property, Sidon subsets and $h$. In particular $S_K$ and $I_K=269^K S_K$ have the same properties.

0.4 *(The finite facts; checked by C1, `v1_facts_O.py`, with the short hand check given in brackets.)*

### Step 1. Levels; which points coincide; $|S_K|=11+9K$
1.1 *(Iterates.)* By F2 and induction, $\varphi^j(x)=L+r^j(x-L)$ for all real $x$ and $j\ge0$. Hence: (A) $251(\varphi^j(x)-L)=r^j y(x)$; (B) if $\varepsilon_1+\dots+\varepsilon_4=0$ then $\sum_i\varepsilon_i\varphi^J(x_i)=r^J\sum_i\varepsilon_ix_i$; (C) $\varphi^J(2u-v)=2\varphi^J(u)-\varphi^J(v)$; (D) $\varphi^J$ is strictly increasing ($r>0$), injective, and maps a 3-AP $\{v<u<w\}$ to the 3-AP $\{\varphi^J(v)<\varphi^J(u)<\varphi^J(w)\}$ with middle $\varphi^J(u)$ (by (B) with $\varepsilon=(1,1,-1,-1)$ applied to $(u,u,v,w)$).

1.2 **Lemma 1.2.** If $x,x'\in O$, $d\ge1$ and $\varphi^d(x')=x$, then $d=1$ and $(x',x)\in\{(0,101),(269,119)\}$; conversely $\varphi(0)=101$, $\varphi(269)=119$.
*Proof.* By 1.1(A), $y(x)=251(x-L)=r^d\,251(x'-L)=r^dy(x')$, i.e. $269^dy(x)=18^dy(x')$. Since 269 is prime and $269\nmid18$ (F1), $269^d\mid y(x')$, and $y(x')\ne0$ (F3). So $269\mid y(x')$, hence $x'\in\{0,269\}$ (F3), and then $y(x')\in\{-101\cdot269,\,150\cdot269\}$ is not divisible by $269^2$ (F3), so $d=1$. Finally $x=\varphi(x')$ and F2 give the two pairs. $\square$

1.3 *(Coincidences between copies.)* Let $0\le j<j'$. A point of copy $j$ $\cap$ copy $j'$ is $\varphi^j(x)=\varphi^{j'}(x')$ with $x,x'\in O$; since $\varphi^j$ is injective, $x=\varphi^{j'-j}(x')$, so by Lemma 1.2 $j'=j+1$ and $(x',x)\in\{(0,101),(269,119)\}$. Conversely $\varphi^{j+1}(0)=\varphi^j(\varphi(0))=\varphi^j(101)$ and $\varphi^{j+1}(269)=\varphi^j(119)$. Hence copy $j$ $\cap$ copy $(j+1)$ $=\{\varphi^j(101),\varphi^j(119)\}=\{\varphi^{j+1}(0),\varphi^{j+1}(269)\}$ (two distinct points, by injectivity of $\varphi^j$), and copies $j,j'$ with $j'\ge j+2$ are disjoint. Within one copy the 11 points $\varphi^j(x)$, $x\in O$, are distinct (injectivity). These are all coincidences among the $11(K+1)$ expressions $\varphi^j(x)$ ($0\le j\le K$, $x\in O$).

1.4 *(Count.)* A point in three copies $j<j'<j''$ would need $j'=j+1$ and $j''=j+1$ (1.3), which is impossible; so every point of $S_K$ lies in $c(p)\in\{1,2\}$ copies. Then $\sum_j|\text{copy }j|=\sum_pc(p)$ and $\sum_{j<j'}|\text{copy }j\cap\text{copy }j'|=\sum_p\binom{c(p)}2$, and $c-\binom c2=1$ for $c\in\{1,2\}$, so $|S_K|=11(K+1)-2K=11+9K$ (the only non-empty intersections are the $K$ consecutive ones, of size 2).

1.5 *(Levels.)* For $p\in S_K$ let $\mathrm{lev}(p)=\min\{j:p\in\text{copy }j\}\le K$ and let $x_p\in O$ be the unique point with $p=\varphi^{\mathrm{lev}(p)}(x_p)$ (unique by injectivity). (i) If $\mathrm{lev}(p)\ge1$ then $x_p\in O^\circ$: if $x_p=0$ then $p=\varphi^{\mathrm{lev}(p)-1}(101)$ lies in an earlier copy, against minimality; the same for $x_p=269$ with $119$. (ii) For $1\le j\le K$ and $x\in O^\circ$, $\mathrm{lev}(\varphi^j(x))=j$: by 1.3 the point lies in no copy $i\le j-2$, and in copy $j-1$ only if it equals $\varphi^j(0)$ or $\varphi^j(269)$, i.e. (injectivity) only if $x\in\{0,269\}$. (iii) So the points of level 0 are the 11 points of $O$, and for $1\le j\le K$ the points of level $j$ are the 9 points $\varphi^j(O^\circ)$ (again $11+9K$ in total); the anchors of copy $j\ge1$ have level $j-1$.

1.6 *(Integers.)* With $N_j(x)=269^j\varphi^j(x)$: $N_0(x)=x$ and $N_j(x)=101\cdot269^j+18N_{j-1}(x)$ (from $\varphi^j(x)=101+18\varphi^{j-1}(x)/269$), so $N_j(x)\in\mathbb Z$ and $I_K=\{269^{K-j}N_j(x):0\le j\le K,\ x\in O\}\subset\mathbb Z$. This proves Theorem 1(a).

### Step 2. Every relation is the AP relation of some $\varphi^J(e)$
Let $(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)$ be a relation in $S_K$, $\varepsilon=(1,1,-1,-1)$, $j_i=\mathrm{lev}(p_i)$, $x_i=x_{p_i}$ (1.5), $J=\max_ij_i\le K$, $T=\{i:j_i=J\}$ (a set of positions) and $m=|T|\in\{1,2,3,4\}$. If $m\le3$ some $j_q<J$, so $J\ge1$ and, by 1.5(i), $x_i\in O^\circ$ for every $i\in T$.

2.1 *(The basic identity.)* $\sum_i\varepsilon_ip_i=0$ and $\sum_i\varepsilon_i=0$ give $\sum_i\varepsilon_i(p_i-L)=0$; by 1.1(A), $\sum_i\varepsilon_ir^{j_i}y(x_i)=0$; multiplying by $269^J$: $\sum_i\varepsilon_i\,18^{j_i}\,269^{J-j_i}\,y(x_i)=0$, an identity between integers.

2.2 *(Reduction mod 269.)* For $i\notin T$ the term contains the factor $269^{J-j_i}$ with $J-j_i\ge1$. Hence $18^J\sum_{i\in T}\varepsilon_iy(x_i)\equiv0\pmod{269}$; as 269 is prime and $269\nmid18$, $\sum_{i\in T}\varepsilon_iy(x_i)\equiv0$; as $y(x)\equiv251x$ and $269\nmid251$ (F1, F3),
$$\textstyle\sum_{i\in T}\varepsilon_ix_i\equiv0\pmod{269}.\qquad(\ast)$$

2.3 *Case $m=1$.* $(\ast)$ says $x_i\equiv0$ for some $x_i\in O^\circ$, impossible by F5(i).

2.4 *Case $m=2$.* If the two positions $i,k$ of $T$ are on opposite sides, $(\ast)$ says $x_i\equiv x_k$, so $x_i=x_k$ (F5(iii)) and $p_i=\varphi^J(x_i)=\varphi^J(x_k)=p_k$ is a point on both sides, impossible by 0.2. If they are on the same side, $(\ast)$ says $\pm(x_i+x_k)\equiv0$ (with $x_i=x_k$ allowed, when $p_i=p_k$), impossible by F5(ii).

2.5 *Case $m=3$.* Exactly one position $q$ has $j_q<J$. The maps $(p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)\mapsto(p_2,p_1,p_3,p_4)$, $(p_1,p_2,p_4,p_3)$, $(p_3,p_4,p_1,p_2)$ send relations to relations with the same point set and the same levels, and they act transitively on the four positions; so we may assume $q=4$, $T=\{1,2,3\}$. Then $(\ast)$ reads $x_1+x_2-x_3\equiv0$ with $x_1,x_2,x_3\in O^\circ$, so by F5(iv) $(x_1,x_2,x_3)=(49,49,98)$ or $(159,159,49)$. Thus $p_1=p_2=\varphi^J(x_1)$, $p_3=\varphi^J(x_3)$, and by 1.1(C) $p_4=p_1+p_2-p_3=2\varphi^J(x_1)-\varphi^J(x_3)=\varphi^J(2x_1-x_3)$, where $2x_1-x_3=0$ resp. $269$. So the relation is $(u,u,v,w)$ with $\{v,u,w\}=\varphi^J(\{0,49,98\})=\varphi^J(e_1)$ (middle $u=\varphi^J(49)$) resp. $\varphi^J(\{49,159,269\})=\varphi^J(e_4)$ (middle $u=\varphi^J(159)$). (This is consistent with $j_4<J$: $\varphi^J(0)$, $\varphi^J(269)$ are anchors of copy $J$, of level $J-1$.)

2.6 *Case $m=4$ (this includes every relation with $J=0$).* All four points have level $J$, so $p_i=\varphi^J(x_i)$ and, by 1.1(B), $0=\sum_i\varepsilon_ip_i=r^J\sum_i\varepsilon_ix_i$. So $(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)$ is a relation in $O$ ($\{x_1,x_2\}\ne\{x_3,x_4\}$ as multisets because $\varphi^J$ is injective and $\{p_1,p_2\}\ne\{p_3,p_4\}$). By F4 it is, up to the symmetries of 2.5, $(u_0,u_0,v_0,w_0)$ with $\{v_0<u_0<w_0\}=e\in E(O)$; applying $\varphi^J$ (1.1(D)) the given relation is $(u,u,v,w)$ with $\{v<u<w\}=\varphi^J(e)$.

2.7 *(Conclusion of Step 2.)* Cases 2.3–2.6 cover all $m$; in every possible case the relation has the form stated in Theorem 1(b), with $J\le K$. In particular its point set has exactly 3 points, so no relation in $S_K$ has 4 distinct points. This proves Theorem 1(b). (No bound on the spread of the levels and no census of a finite $S_{K_0}$ is needed; a posteriori the levels in a relation differ by at most 1.)

### Step 3. The AP hypergraph
3.1 *($\supseteq$.)* For $e\in E(O)$ and $0\le J\le K$, $\varphi^J(e)$ is a 3-AP (1.1(D)) contained in copy $J\subseteq S_K$.

3.2 *($\subseteq$.)* A 3-AP $\{v<u<w\}\subseteq S_K$ gives the relation $(u,u,v,w)$ ($\{u,u\}\ne\{v,w\}$ since $v\ne u$); by Step 2 its point set $\{v,u,w\}$ equals $\varphi^J(e)$ for some $J\le K$, $e\in E(O)$. So $H(S_K)=\{\varphi^J(e)\}$.

3.3 *(Distinct, one copy each.)* $\varphi^J(e)=\varphi^J(e')$ forces $e=e'$ (injectivity). For $J<J'$, $\varphi^J(e)\subseteq$ copy $J$ and $\varphi^{J'}(e')\subseteq$ copy $J'$, which share at most 2 points (1.3), so the two 3-sets differ, and no edge lies in two copies. Hence $|H(S_K)|=9(K+1)$, and copy $J$ contains exactly the nine edges $\varphi^J(E(O))$.

3.4 *(Linearity.)* Two distinct edges $\varphi^J(e)$, $\varphi^{J'}(e')$ with $J\le J'$ share at most one point: if $J=J'$, $|\varphi^J(e)\cap\varphi^J(e')|=|e\cap e'|\le1$ (F4); if $J'=J+1$, common points lie in copy $J$ $\cap$ copy $(J+1)=\{\varphi^{J+1}(0),\varphi^{J+1}(269)\}$, and if both were in $\varphi^{J+1}(e')$ then $\{0,269\}\subseteq e'$ (injectivity), excluded by F4; if $J'\ge J+2$ the copies are disjoint. With F4 (middles) this proves Theorem 1(c). (Since $\varphi([0,269])=[101,119]$, $S_K\subset[0,269]$ has extremes $0,269$; the $9(K+1)=|S_K|-2$ middles $\varphi^J(u)$, $u\in O^\circ$, are exactly the non-extreme points, each once: $S_K$ is midpoint-saturated.)

### Step 4. The (4,5)-property
Let $B\subseteq S_K$, $|B|=4$; its 6 two-element subsets ("pairs") $\{s,t\}$ have lengths $|s-t|>0$.

4.1 Two distinct pairs of equal length share a point, and then form a 3-AP inside $B$ whose middle is the shared point. Indeed, if $\{s,t\},\{s',t'\}$ are disjoint, $s,t,s',t'$ are four distinct points and $s-t=\pm(s'-t')$ gives $s+t'=t+s'$ or $s+s'=t+t'$, a relation on four distinct points, excluded by Step 2. If they share a point, write them $\{u,v\},\{u,w\}$ with $v\ne w$: $u-v=u-w$ is impossible, so $u-v=w-u$, i.e. $v+w=2u$ and $\{v,u,w\}$ is a 3-AP with middle $u$.

4.2 No three distinct pairs have equal length. By 4.1 they pairwise intersect, so they form a star $\{u,v\},\{u,w\},\{u,z\}$ (three pairwise intersecting 2-sets without a common point would form a triangle $\{u,v\},\{v,w\},\{u,w\}$) or a triangle. A star is impossible: only $u\pm\delta$ have distance $\delta$ from $u$, not three distinct points. A triangle is impossible: of three distinct reals, the largest distance is the sum of the other two, which are positive.

4.3 Suppose $B$ had at most 4 distinct lengths. By 4.2 the 6 pairs fall into $c_1$ classes of size 1 and $c_2$ classes of size 2 with $c_1+2c_2=6$ and $c_1+c_2\le4$, so $c_2\ge2$. By 4.1 a class of size 2 is $\{\{u,v\},\{u,w\}\}$ for a 3-AP $\{v,u,w\}\subseteq B$ with middle $u$, and it is determined by that 3-set (its middle is its middle element); so two classes give two different 3-APs inside the 4-set $B$, which share exactly two points. This contradicts linearity (3.4). Hence every 4-subset determines at least 5 distinct positive differences, i.e. $|B-B|\ge11$ (0.1), and by 0.3 the same holds for $I_K$. This proves Theorem 1(d).

### Step 5. Sidon subsets are chains of per-copy choices; the transfer matrix; $h(S_K)\le7+5K$
5.1 *(Sidon = no edge.)* $T\subseteq S_K$ is Sidon iff $T$ contains no edge of $H(S_K)$. If $\{v<u<w\}\subseteq T$ is an edge, then $(u,u)$ and $(v,w)$ are distinct pairs with equal sums. Conversely, if $T$ is not Sidon, two distinct pairs $(a\le b)$, $(c\le d)$ of $T$ have $a+b=c+d$, a relation in $S_K$ with all points in $T$, whose point set is an edge (Step 2). (This is Ma–Tang's Lemma 2.3 (K4) for these sets, proved directly.)

5.2 *(Coordinates.)* For $T\subseteq S_K$ let $U_j=\varphi^{-j}(T\cap\text{copy }j)\subseteq O$, $0\le j\le K$. Call $U\subseteq O$ *admissible* if it contains no member of $E(O)$ (by F4 this is the same as: $U$ is Sidon). Since every edge $\varphi^J(e)$ lies in copy $J$, $\varphi^J(e)\subseteq T$ iff $e\subseteq U_J$; with 5.1 and 3.2, **$T$ is Sidon iff every $U_j$ is admissible.**

5.3 *(Consistency, bijection, size.)* For $U\subseteq O$ put $a(U)=([0\in U],[269\in U])$ (anchor state) and $b(U)=([101\in U],[119\in U])$ (exit state), in $\{0,1\}^2$, and $|s|=s_1+s_2$. Because $\varphi^j(101)=\varphi^{j+1}(0)$ and $\varphi^j(119)=\varphi^{j+1}(269)$ are the same points, $b(U_j)=a(U_{j+1})$ for $0\le j<K$ ("consistency"). Conversely every consistent tuple $(U_0,\dots,U_K)$ of subsets of $O$ comes from exactly one $T$, namely $T=\bigcup_j\varphi^j(U_j)$: by definition $\varphi^j(U_j)\subseteq T\cap\text{copy }j$; a point of $T\cap$ copy $j$ lies in some $\varphi^i(U_i)$; $|i-j|\ge2$ is impossible (disjoint copies); if $i=j+1$ it is $\varphi^{j+1}(z)$ with $z\in U_{j+1}\cap\{0,269\}$, i.e. $\varphi^j(101)$ or $\varphi^j(119)$ with $101$ resp. $119\in U_j$ by consistency; if $i=j-1$ it is $\varphi^{j-1}(z)$ with $z\in U_{j-1}\cap\{101,119\}$, i.e. $\varphi^j(0)$ or $\varphi^j(269)$ with $0$ resp. $269\in U_j$; so $T\cap$ copy $j=\varphi^j(U_j)$. By 1.5(iii) the points of $T$ of level 0 are $U_0$ and those of level $j\ge1$ are $\varphi^j(U_j\cap O^\circ)$, so
$$\textstyle|T|=|U_0|+\sum_{j=1}^K\big(|U_j|-|a(U_j)|\big).$$
Hence $h(S_K)$ is the maximum of this expression over consistent tuples of admissible sets.

5.4 *(Transfer matrix.)* For $s,s'\in\{0,1\}^2$ let $\mathrm{best}(s,s')=\max\{|U|:U\subseteq O\text{ admissible},\ a(U)=s,\ b(U)=s'\}$ (all 16 entries are finite: no member of $E(O)$ lies inside $\{0,101,119,269\}$, because the only member meeting this set twice is $e_7=\{83,101,119\}$; so every subset of $\{0,101,119,269\}$ is admissible, and these subsets realise all 16 state pairs), $M[s][s']=\mathrm{best}(s,s')-|s|$, $R_0[s']=\max_s\mathrm{best}(s,s')$. For $k\ge0$ let $R_k[s']$ be the maximum of $|U_0|+\sum_{j=1}^k(|U_j|-|a(U_j)|)$ over consistent tuples $(U_0,\dots,U_k)$ of admissible sets with $b(U_k)=s'$. Splitting such a tuple into $(U_0,\dots,U_{k-1})$ with $b(U_{k-1})=s$ and $U_k$ with $a(U_k)=s$, $b(U_k)=s'$ (the two parts are constrained only through $s$, and the objective is additive) gives the max-plus recursion
$$R_k[s']=\max_s\big(R_{k-1}[s]+M[s][s']\big)=:(R_{k-1}\otimes M)[s'],\qquad h(S_K)=\max_{s'}R_K[s'] .$$

5.5 *(The table, C2; states ordered $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$; rows $s$, columns $s'$.)*
$$\mathrm{best}=\begin{pmatrix}5&6&6&6\\5&6&6&6\\5&6&6&6\\6&7&7&7\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}5&6&6&6\\4&5&5&5\\4&5&5&5\\4&5&5&5\end{pmatrix},\qquad R_0=(6,7,7,7).$$

Max-plus multiplication is translation-equivariant, $(R+c)\otimes M=(R\otimes M)+c$, so by induction $R_k=R_0+5k$ for all $k$, and $h(S_K)=\max R_K=7+5K$. For the upper bound $h(S_K)\le7+5K$ only the inequalities $M\le M^+$, $R_0\le R_0^+$ entrywise are needed, where $M^+$, $R_0^+$ denote the displayed values: max-plus multiplication is monotone, so $R_K\le R_0^+\otimes M^+\otimes\cdots\otimes M^+=R_0^++5K$.
